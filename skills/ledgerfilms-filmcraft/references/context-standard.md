# The Context Standard — what “matched” actually means

Source: the user's verdict on the AI Sell-Off long-form (2026-11-09, 5/10, “still lack of context”) and his benchmark, FactZaara's “How Crude Oil Travels From the Ocean Floor to a Giant Tanker Ship” (17M views, 13:54), which he called perfect for story + voiceover + visual matching. Full analysis (timed transcript + 150-shot breakdown) lives at `~/workspace/refanalysis/`. These rules tighten the one law: the screen shows what the voice says — *literally, mechanically, and locatably*.

## Why the AI Sell-Off film failed this standard despite “matching” clips
Its shots depicted each line's *mood and metaphor* (a red line for the market, three symbolic characters, figure overlays for the numbers). The viewer still had to decode symbols and take every mechanism on faith. The benchmark never asks that: it shows the thing itself, the mechanism itself, and the map of where we are — in that order, every time. Cutting pace was not the gap (5.1s/clip vs the benchmark's 5.6s/shot). The gap is referent literalism, mechanism visibility, and tour-guide narration.

## The rules

1. **Literal referent rule.** Every noun the narration names is on screen as itself within the same beat — the actual machine, place, document, person-type, or physical state. A symbol or mood tableau may *accompany* a fact, never *carry* it. If the referent cannot be pictured, the line is rewritten until it can (existing law, now enforced at the noun level).
2. **Hidden-mechanism rule.** When the mechanism is invisible (underground, inside a machine, inside a market, inside a law), the screenplay plans a DIAGRAM beat of that exact hidden space — cross-section, cutaway, flow diagram — and the process is shown *moving* inside it (the benchmark: the drill string descends through labelled strata to the red reservoir; the well path curves; the hull cutaway shows the tanks). A figure overlay is not a mechanism. No mechanism is ever left to narration alone.
3. **System-map opener.** The film's world is established as ONE locatable system early — a single frame/diagram that shows all the places the story will visit and how they connect (the benchmark's first frame is the cross-section of ocean → seabed → reservoir; it returns to the system view at chapter turns). Recurring locations in a LedgerFilms story (exchange, data centre, grid, town) get the same treatment: the viewer can always answer “where are we in the system?”
4. **Tour-guide narration.** The voice speaks like a guide standing next to the viewer, not an essayist: deictic language (“this tower”, “here, inside this drum”), signposted chapter turns (“Now we come to…”, “But where does it wait?”), questions the next shot answers, and an analogy anchored to an everyday object for every abstract mechanism (gas injection = blowing air into a bottle to push the water out). One fact per line; a line that packs three facts gets three lines and three shots.
5. **Human-anchor rule.** Scale and stakes are translated into human terms *and shown*: comparisons to known objects (taller than a named landmark), the people who do the work shown as faces and routines (shifts, cabins, control rooms), the danger shown (storm seas while the voice says storms kill). A human context shot is not decoration rationed per sequence — it is how every big number earns its meaning.
6. **Viewer's-life bookends.** The hook starts from the viewer's own daily life (your car, your home, your phone) and the payoff returns to exactly that spot (the petrol-pump callback), so the whole mechanism is framed as the hidden backstory of something the viewer touches daily. LedgerFilms equivalent: open and close on the viewer's money life (the bill, the app, the rent, the price at the till), not inside the market's own world.
7. **Pointers and labels.** Diagrams and real shots carry graphic pointers — arrows, labels, highlights — that land on the exact part being named as it is named. In LedgerFilms production these are planned GRAPHIC beats in the screenplay (shapes in-clip + editor overlays), not finishing-pass decoration.
8. **Chapter signposts + climax marking.** The narration names its own structure (“the most epic moment of this video is…”), and chapters are separated by a visible change of place/stage in the journey. The spine is ONE thing moving through space (a barrel of oil: seabed → well → separator → tank → tanker → refinery → pump). A LedgerFilms story must find its equivalent moving thing (a dollar, a megawatt, a vote, a chip) and let the camera follow it; if nothing moves through the story, the story is restructured until something does.

## Blocking gate (add to the correlation gate)
**Context gate:** for any 5 random narration lines, a viewer watching muted can answer three questions from the picture alone: (1) WHAT object/place is this line about? (2) WHERE is it in the film's system? (3) WHAT is happening to it / how does it work? Any line that fails any question returns the screenplay to scripting — the fix is a literal shot or a diagram beat, never a mood shot and never an overlay alone.
